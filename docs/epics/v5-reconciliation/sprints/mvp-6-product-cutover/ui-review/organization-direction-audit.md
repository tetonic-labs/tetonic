**Tetonic: directing a digital organization — product and HCI audit**

September 28, 2026. Audit and proposals only; no application changes.

**Tetonic has a promising visual foundation, but its current information model asks the operator to infer organizational progress from individual activity.** The next design step should make outcomes, dependencies, and decisions explicit. Further animation or contrast work alone cannot close that gap.

I inspected the React implementation and walked through the original workspace (three agents on its initial team map), the 18-agent Studio incident example, and the 80-agent Network workspace with 10 teams and 24 resources. I exercised the rush and cross-team delivery examples, selected agents, inspected profiles and activity, opened tool evidence and approval requests, and changed team scope. I inspected the crowded view at 1280×720 and 390×844. Trace inspection covered representative early transitions, not full 29–35 minute runs. No real execution commands or approval decisions were submitted.

This is an expert inspection of a local preview, not a usability study or an assessment of the engine's production reliability. Observations below distinguish interface behavior from predicted user effects. The preview explicitly says no engine is connected. Missing UI contracts do not establish that corresponding engine capabilities are absent.

**The existing strengths are worth preserving.** Stable team neighborhoods and stationary portraits provide orientation. A selected agent remains selected as work changes, related entities receive emphasis, and selection does not chase the agent with the camera. Shared agents have one identity on the organization map. Explicit team exploration and fit-to-map provide recovery. The warm palette, recognizable faces, restrained controls, and typography make the product approachable. Approval requests explain their reason, requested action, and origin, with additional evidence available on demand. Named controls, keyboard camera navigation, modal focus restoration, and reduced-motion support are useful foundations, although I did not perform a full accessibility conformance assessment.

These strengths reduce visual effort. They do not yet answer the organizational questions: Are we achieving the intended result? What could prevent it? Which decision is mine? What happened after I intervened?

The diagnosis uses visibility, recognition, consistency, and recovery as inspection lenses, as described in [Nielsen's usability heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/). Clear capability boundaries, explainable behavior, and support for correction are also relevant to this product's AI interactions; see [Microsoft's human–AI interaction guidance](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/). The specific findings and proposals here come from Tetonic's implementation and walkthroughs, not claims that those sources validate this particular design.

**The five most consequential problems, in priority order:**

1. **Attention signals do not agree about what needs the operator.** In Network → All-hands rush, advance three interaction states and select Ada 1. The focus card reports an unresolved Terminal 1 failure, while the global control reports zero requests. Opening it says “Nothing needs you.” Opening Ada's profile says “Ready”; Activity → All events contains zero sample events. This is directly observed, and reflects separate playback, approval, and message stores. The likely consequence is false reassurance or repeated checking across views. A failed operation does not automatically require a human, but its recovery owner and effect must be explicit. Use one exception lifecycle: detected, recovering, waiting for judgment, resolved, with evidence. As an immediate wording correction, an approval-only inbox should promise only that there are no pending approvals. Also avoid clearing a failure merely because a later operation at the same resource succeeded; current playback does that without establishing that it was the relevant retry. 2. **The main representation describes activity more clearly than purpose or progress.** The rush shows counts of active agents and resource interactions. Cross-team delivery selection shows a label such as “Workstream 1 / Discover: branch 1,” its destination, and recent starts. There is no visible initiative owner, acceptance criterion, verified milestone, dependency explanation, or impact of delay. The frontend types model agents, teams, approvals, events, and tracks; they do not supply an organizational objective/decision contract. The likely consequence is mistaking busyness for progress and having to reconstruct the plan mentally. Make an outcome a navigable object with an accountable owner, constraints, evidence of completion, next milestone, and explicit unknowns. Do not derive a completion percentage from event volume. Preserve the map for the questions it answers well: who is involved, where relationships cross teams, and which resources are shared. [Frontend model](</C:/Users/Mark Miller/Desktop/repos/lokai/web/src/types/index.ts:1>), [recent workflow projection](</C:/Users/Mark Miller/Desktop/repos/lokai/web/src/components/graph/TeamActivityMap.tsx:178>).

3. **Giving direction lacks a visible execution agreement.** The composer accepts free text for a team or individual. Messages remain local events, and the preview correctly discloses that no engine ran. There is no product interaction yet for interpreting an instruction, identifying its scope, previewing affected work, acknowledging acceptance, or checking the resulting outcome. In the All teams view, the recipient still says “Team,” leaving its intended breadth ambiguous. Pause controls refer to preview behavior rather than operational stopping. These are missing capabilities, not failed live operations. Before connecting real execution, design a compact direction receipt: what was understood, who owns it, which work changes, which constraints apply, and how success will be checked. A consequential reprioritization needs a consequence preview; an ordinary question should not require another approval ritual. Show requested, accepted, applied, and verified as distinct states. Never imply that an irreversible action can be undone merely because a command can be cancelled. 4. **Drilling down loses the continuity needed to investigate and resume work.** The delivery focus card contains at most three start events; the profile, Activity view, and resource inspector read other snapshots. Switching from the running organization example to Research cleared selection and restarted playback. Research is labeled eight agents on the organization map but sixteen in Teams, because the map counts primary homes while the directory includes shared members; the distinction is not named. Selecting Research then shows both Engineering and Research neighborhoods. Those behaviors are defensible internally but require the user to reconcile incompatible-looking contexts. Preserve a navigation trail and the selected workflow, view position, filters, and temporal context. Label primary membership and shared contribution separately. Use one current-state projection with links into durable evidence. Returning after an absence should show material changes since the last review, including uncertainty and unresolved decisions. The current preview pauses when hidden and stores most work state only in memory, so it cannot validate that return journey. 5. **The distant view reduces detail without consistently preserving decision relevance.** Overview routes are the six highest-volume bundles. A quiet but consequential dependency can therefore be absent; selection helps only after someone knows what to select. The team summary puts attention information after agent/activity counts, where it can be truncated. On the measured 390×844 Network view, portrait buttons were approximately 9.3×9.3 screen pixels, while the 220×260 focus card covered much of two team columns. The likely effects are missed exceptions, difficult targeting, and orientation loss. These are not solved by strengthening every mark. At organization scale, make team/initiative summaries the primary targets; retain meaningful exceptions regardless of traffic volume, state that routine routes are summarized, and provide accessible aggregate-to-detail navigation. Give failure/decision indicators a protected position that cannot be ellipsized away. On narrow screens, use a dedicated detail view with a clear return path, or a sheet whose relationship to the underlying selection stays legible. **The proposed interaction model is a connected decision loop.** The operator establishes intent, delegates within explicit boundaries, supervises meaningful changes, intervenes when judgment is needed, and verifies the result. Teams and agents remain accessible throughout, but they are not the only way to find work.

| Level | Question it should answer | Information to show first | Path to the next level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Organization | What matters now, and what requires my judgment? | A short, stable set of outcomes; material changes; decisions ranked by consequence and time sensitivity; freshness and unknowns | Select an outcome or decision; inspect the organization map for relationships |
| Workflow | What is blocking this result, and what happens next? | Accepted milestones, current stage, dependency chain, responsible owner, next commitment, exceptions, evidence | Select a blocked dependency, decision, or contribution |
| Intervention | What change can I make, with what consequences? | Exact scope, affected work and owners, constraints, alternatives, reversible versus irreversible effects | Apply direction, then inspect acceptance and execution receipt |
| Evidence | Why should I believe this status? | Relevant artifacts, events, approvals, timestamps, provenance, unresolved contradictions | Return to the same decision/workflow position |

A shared agent should retain a single identity and home. A workflow can show that agent's contribution without creating a second person. Membership does not imply availability: show competing commitments and accountable ownership when they affect the selected result. If capacity is unknown, say so.

State, causal explanation, and forecast should be distinguishable. “Build failed” is an observation; “release is blocked by this failure” needs dependency evidence; “release may slip two hours” is an estimate that needs assumptions. These should not be flattened into one confident status sentence.

**Three design directions are worth comparing with prototypes.** The walkthroughs below are proposed scenarios, not existing capabilities.

| Direction | Concrete walkthrough | Benefit and attention cost | Tradeoff and failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome-led workroom | Start with “Release candidate: waiting on security review.” Open it to see accepted milestones, the blocked branch, and its owner. Inspect the shared reviewer commitment. Defer a lower-priority initiative, review the consequences, and receive acknowledgment of the revised plan. | Organizes attention around the result; the operator need not inspect every worker. Requires maintaining a trustworthy hierarchy and exceptions. | Poorly defined outcomes can become vague cards. Non-project operations need ongoing service commitments rather than forced completion bars. |
| Exception-led briefing | Start with one decision: “Security review capacity is blocking two initiatives.” Inspect the affected work, compare options, choose a priority, and watch for confirmation. Routine activity stays quiet, with a periodic health/changes review. | Small reading surface and fast triage. Requires reviewing the system's prioritization and occasionally sampling apparently healthy work. | An incomplete detector can create false reassurance. Reactive work can crowd out strategic planning, and an alert inbox can become another endless queue. |
| Map-led organization with team briefs | Enter through stable team regions showing commitments and actionable exceptions. Select Research to see its contribution to the release; follow a highlighted dependency to Security; adjust ownership in that workflow's detail. | Preserves the current spatial character and helps relationship-heavy investigation. Requires more scanning and navigation to synthesize the whole organization. | Distant labels and cross-team complexity remain constraints. Teams can obscure outcome ownership, and mobile spatial navigation remains expensive. |

**I recommend an outcome-led workroom, with an exception-led briefing inside it and the current map available as a relationship view.** It connects priorities, delegation, intervention, and verification in one path. The exception-led alternative is stronger for routine triage but incomplete for setting direction. The map-led alternative preserves the strongest existing visual identity but still asks the user to infer organizational intent from geography.

This should not become a dashboard packed with cards. Start with a restrained editorial list: the few outcomes the operator is supervising, what changed, and the decisions that could alter them. Each row earns its place through consequence, not throughput. Keep order stable during reading; surface new urgent items without moving the target under the pointer. Let an explicit “review latest” action reconcile changes while a user studies an older state, and always display the age of that state. Preserve Tetonic's warmth, portraits, generous space, and copper emphasis. Use motion only to explain a meaningful transition or confirm an intervention.

For a small team with one outcome, this can collapse to a single workroom and composer. A large organization should expose more abstraction rather than more permanent controls. On mobile, prioritize the briefing, a decision, and its evidence; keep the map available without making precise portrait selection necessary.

The recommendation has real risks. A summary can omit the weak signal that matters. A stale dependency graph can make a sensible-looking priority change harmful. Outcome ownership can be ambiguous when several teams contribute. An apparently reassuring “on track” state can reflect absent telemetry. Counter these with traceable claims, freshness, explicit unknowns, visible coverage boundaries, conflict reporting, and a way to inspect all unresolved exceptions. Allow the user to reject an inferred dependency or correct ownership without editing a graph of every event.

One person can only direct an organization if routine choices are actually delegated. Interface clarity cannot compensate for thousands of independent decisions that all require that person's judgment. Establish operating constraints, escalation conditions, budgets where applicable, and accountable owners. Track the human decision backlog and age alongside the number of agents; the practical scaling limit is the unresolved judgment burden.

**The sequence should start with truthful, connected state, then test the new interaction model.**

| Order | Work | What to remove or simplify | Acceptance evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate: make the current preview internally coherent | Narrow the approval inbox's empty-state claim; carry a selected sample interaction into its evidence; distinguish recovery from required decisions; protect attention text; label home versus shared membership | Conflicting ready/failure messages, attention counts hidden after routine counts, unrelated snapshots presented as a continuous story | The same injected failure has compatible status and trace links in overview, focus, profile, and inbox |
| Immediate: preserve investigation context | Keep scope navigation separate from simulation reset; preserve selection and return position; clarify All teams composer scope and preview-pause semantics | Unrequested resets, ambiguous “Team” addressing, tiny individual targets as the only route to detail | Enter and exit a team, profile, and approval without losing the selected workflow or time context |
| Prototype: outcome and workflow experience | Build one end-to-end release scenario with parallel branches, a shared resource conflict, evidence-backed milestones, and a consequential priority change | Event counts as a proxy for progress; generic workstream labels as the main story | An operator can explain the blocker and choose a scoped intervention without reading raw event logs |
| Prototype: attention and return journey | Add a briefing with deduplicated exceptions, explicit recovery ownership, “changed since review,” acknowledgment, and resolution | Every retry becoming a new alert; a generic green/all-clear state when data is missing | One persistent issue survives retries; an important change is recognized after an interruption |
| Structural: connect the product contract to the engine | Map durable outcome, dependency, contribution, decision, command, and evidence identities to actual capabilities; supply timestamps, acknowledgments, freshness, permissions, and stop semantics | UI-only success receipts presented as execution; scattered local sources of operational truth | Requested, accepted, applied, failed, and verified states are distinguishable and recover after reconnect |

Before expanding navigation, test the smallest coherent slice: one outcome, one blocked dependency, one decision, one scoped intervention, one verified result. Make that journey understandable with both 4 and 80 agents. Then introduce multiple outcomes and competing priorities. Do not build three new permanent workspaces merely because three prototype directions were considered.

**Validation should measure judgment, not just navigation speed.** Recruit six to eight representative participants for an initial formative round: technical operators, cross-team project leads, and founders or generalists who would delegate substantial work. Include keyboard-first and reduced-motion use. That sample can reveal problems; it cannot establish population-wide performance claims.

Compare the current preview with an outcome-led prototype using equivalent, ground-truthed scenarios. Counterbalance order to reduce learning effects. Build coherent test fixtures that connect the same goals, approvals, failures, messages, and evidence; the current busy traces are useful visual stress tests but do not yet represent a unified management scenario. Test sparse, medium, and crowded organizations, with long names, uneven teams, shared agents, unassigned work, stale telemetry, and rare critical dependencies amid heavy routine traffic.

| Scenario | What the participant must do | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Start the day | Identify the highest-consequence issue and justify it | Time to the correct priority, missed critical issues, irrelevant inspections |
| Direct a cross-team initiative | Establish an outcome, boundaries, owner, and completion evidence | Correct delegation scope; ability to restate the accepted plan |
| Busy but stalled | Explain why a busy release is not progressing | Causal accuracy; time to the true dependency; reliance on raw logs |
| Competing priorities | Reallocate a shared agent/resource without breaking a higher priority | Impact prediction; incorrect scope; downstream effects overlooked |
| Failure and approval burst | Distinguish auto-recovery from decisions needing judgment | Missed decisions, duplicate handling, unnecessary intervention |
| Focus amid ongoing work | Inspect one workflow while other work changes | Orientation loss, off-target actions, awareness of a new important exception |
| Change direction | Pause or redirect the intended scope and verify acceptance | Scope errors; time to acknowledgment; confusion between accepted and completed |
| Return after interruption | Resume after a five-minute distractor, then inspect a simulated several-hour gap | Time to recover context, recalled versus recognized changes, unresolved work overlooked |

Provisional prototype targets—not measured results—are: at least 90% correct critical-issue identification, at least 90% successful scoped interventions, no wrong-scope consequential actions in the formative round, and approximately 30% less time than baseline to identify the true blocker. Investigate every high-impact miss individually. Adjust targets after establishing baseline and realistic task difficulty.

After each task, ask participants to explain what is happening, what they believe will happen next, and how confident they are. Compare those answers with scenario truth. High confidence with an incorrect explanation is a worse result than visible uncertainty. Record perceived mental effort, repeated checks, backtracking, and time spent monitoring without making a decision. Re-test with injected missing or contradictory telemetry so the design is evaluated under uncertainty, not only when summaries are correct.

For accessibility, verify focus order and restoration, useful group navigation before 80 individual controls, persistent non-color exception cues, target sizes, text resizing, screen-reader announcements, and reduced-motion behavior. For the eventual connected product, test stale commands, duplicate submission, partial acceptance, reconnect, and recovery separately from this visual prototype.

The strongest next experiment is a complete management loop that the operator can explain: **what we are trying to achieve, what changed, why it matters, what I can do, and whether my decision worked.**
